Abstract. Neural models yield high accuracy for their predictions but
lack adequate reasoning. Rationalization is a method that aims to make

neural models inherently interpretable. It first selects a short and mean-
ingful part of the input text, called a rationale, and then uses it to make

the final prediction. This architecture aims to mimic human-style rea-
soning by justifying predictions with textual evidence. However, in unsu-
pervised settings, models tend to exploit spurious correlations to achieve

high accuracy and generate rationales that often lack faithfulness and
plausibility. To overcome this limitation, noise-based training strategies
have been introduced that selectively replace non-critical tokens with
random words from the vocabulary with a probability determined by
the noise parameter, forcing the model to identify genuinely meaningful

input segments. Building on this, we introduce Adaptive Noise Injec-
tion (ANI), which injects noise aggressively at the start of training and

gradually reduces, encouraging the model to explore diverse explanations
and avoid early reliance on superficial cues. The approach improves the
faithfulness and human-likeness of rationales while maintaining strong

predictive performance, advancing the development of inherently inter-
pretable NLP systems.

1 Introduction
With advancements in pretrained large language models, the transparency and
complexity of the models have increased drastically. Interpreting the neural

model decision solely based on attention weights yields an implausible expla-
nation. Rationalization aims to improve the transparency of neural models by

first generating a concise and sufficient subset of the input text, called ratio-
nales, and, second, making predictions with the rationales generated in place of

the original text, as proposed in Lei et al. [1]. However, the unsupervised nature
of rationale generation often leads the model to generate implausible, unfaithful,
and incomprehensible rationales. Online Noise Injection, introduced in Storek
et al. [2], aims to address this by perturbing the generated rationale. It seeks to

and predictor are optimized jointly, the generator may exploit shortcut cues or
attend to irrelevant tokens that correlate with the prediction label, leading to
overfitting or spurious rationales.
In this work, we introduce Adaptive Noise Injection (ANI) to adapt the
noise injection strategy to the dynamic nature of neural model’s training. ANI
dynamically injects noise during different stages of training. During the initial
stage of training, the probability of replacing a non-relevant token with a random
token based on ATF-IDF is high. This prevents the model from catching spurious
patterns early on during training and helps in exploring diverse explanations.
As the training proceeds further, the noise injection probability decays and the
model converges towards generating plausible and comprehensive rationales.
2 Related Work

Research in the field of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) can be catego-
rized into two methods: post hoc and ante hoc. The post hoc method achieves

explainability after the model is trained. Ante hoc methods are designed to incor-
porate interpretability into the model during training. The generator-predictor

paradigm is an ante hoc method, introduced in Lei et al. [1].

Towards Differentiable Rationale Extraction: Although the rationale extraction

approach proposed by Lei et al. [1] marked an important step forward in ex-
plainable models, it faced notable limitations. The gradient estimation method,

REINFORCE Williams [4], is well known for its high variance in gradient esti-
mates, often leading to unstable and inefficient optimization. As a result, training

such models became challenging, particularly when aiming for robust and prac-
tical deployment. To overcome these obstacles, further research proposed a fully

differentiable framework by introducing the HardKuma distribution Bastings
et al. [5]. The HardKuma distribution serves as a smooth reparameterization
approximation to the Bernoulli distribution.

A2R: Attention to Rationale Architecture: Building on previous work in ratio-
nale extraction, the A2R framework introduced in Yu et al. [3] is the first to

incorporate large language models to generate rationales in an end-to-end differ-
entiable manner. A2R leverages large models’ continuous soft attention outputs

to guide rationale selection, allowing for more stable and effective training. The
generator module incorporates a non-trainable embedding layer initialized with
GloVe vectors Pennington et al. [6], followed by a linear layer that assigns scores

to individual tokens. A2R is structured around three core components: a ratio-
nale generator, a rationale-based predictor, and an attention-based predictor.

which simultaneously serves as an attention mechanism and a distribution from

which a binary rationale mask M is sampled, with M ∼ α(X). The rationale-

Unsupervised Selective Rationalization with Noise Injection Storek et al. [2] im-
proves the A2R architecture by proposing a creative and effective technique

called Online Noise Injection. Although A2R relies on a soft attention-based ra-
tionale mask and joint training with an attention-based predictor, this newer

approach strengthens the quality of the rationale by explicitly penalizing unin-
formative token selections. The key idea is to replace tokens that are deemed

less important in the generated rationale with randomly sampled words from

the vocabulary. This discourages the generator from selecting irrelevant or triv-
ial tokens, such as stop words, since doing so would introduce noise and hinder

the predictor’s performance. As a result, the model learns to identify rationales
that are not only compact and interpretable but also genuinely influential for

prediction. Yu et al. [3] explores how selective rationalization models can be-
come trapped in suboptimal solutions during training, whereas Atanasova et al.

[8] introduce a training approach guided by diagnostic feedback to enhance the

plausibility of model explanations. To effectively exploit the noise injection mech-
anism, the original low-capacity generator with BERT Devlin et al. [9] has been

substituted, enabling the use of a less complex, attention-driven predictor com-
pared to A2R.

Despite its advantages in promoting plausibility and faithfulness, the use of
noise injection in this framework introduces a key limitation: the reliance on a
fixed noise probability p throughout the training. This static configuration fails
to adapt to the evolving capabilities of the model during training. In the early
stages, when the rationale generator is still learning to identify relevant tokens, a
high level of noise may excessively disrupt the learning signal, making it difficult
for the model to converge. In contrast, when the generator has improved during
the later stages of training, a fixed noise level may no longer provide sufficient
regularization to encourage further refinement of the rationales. Thus, the lack
of a dynamic or adaptive mechanism to control the intensity of noise injection
can lead to suboptimal learning dynamics, hindering the model’s ability to fully
exploit the benefits of noise-based regularization.

3 Methodology

Fig. 2. Architecture of Unsupervised Selective Rationalization with Noise Injection,
Storek et al. [2] introduced the idea of Online Noise Injection to perturb the rationales
generated, but relied on an optimal noise value p. We replace the static noise input
with a dynamic noise injection mechanism that varies at different stages of training.
Inspired by previous work on selective rationalization and attention-guided
rationale generation of Storek et al. [2] and Yu et al. [3], respectively, we propose

Adaptive Noise Injection (ANI) to dynamically inject noise into rationale gener-
ated at different stages of training. The central hypothesis is that if a rationale

remains predictive even after controlled corruption, then it must encode infor-
mation aligned with human interpretability rather than latent, model-specific

artifacts.
Unlike fixed noise strategies, which apply a constant perturbation level, ANI
integrates a progressively decreasing noise schedule analogous to curriculum
learning. In the early stages of training, higher levels of injected noise prevent the
generator–predictor system from prematurely converging on brittle token–label
correlations. This design choice is motivated by observations from Jacovi and
Goldberg [10] and similar degeneracy findings in explainable NLP systems, where
the generator may initially latch onto narrow or overly specific lexical cues that
appear predictive, yet lack semantic grounding. By applying substantial noise

early in training, ANI enforces exploration of a wider space of potential ratio-
nales, encouraging the model to learn stable, generalizable explanatory struc-
tures rather than memorized token-level associations.

As training progresses, the noise level gradually decays towards an optimized

lower threshold. This controlled reduction allows the model to consolidate learn-
ing around meaningful patterns once its representational space has matured. In

later epochs, low-noise conditions ensure that the predictor fine-tunes its reliance
on coherent, information-bearing rationale tokens rather than being forced to
compensate for artificially disrupted input. This progressive adjustment mirrors
annealing strategies commonly used in reinforcement learning and variational

6 Mano et al.
inference, where exploration dominates early learning, and exploitation becomes

beneficial once the model has acquired a sufficiently structured latent represen-
tation.

Through this dynamic modulation, ANI strikes a balance between prevent-
ing the fomation of degenerate rationale early in training and supporting the

convergence toward faithful explanatory behavior in later stages. As a result,
the final learned rationales not only contribute to an accurate prediction but
also exhibit improved alignment with human-interpretable evidence, achieving
stronger justification quality without sacrificing predictive performance.

5 Evaluation Metrics

The rationales generated by the model are evaluated using a variety of met-
rics designed to assess both faithfulness and plausibility. Faithfulness measures

how well the rationale genuinely explains the model’s decision, while plausibility
captures how closely the generated rationale aligns with human expectations.
5.1 Faithfulness

Following DeYoung et al. [11], faithfulness is assessed using two metrics: com-
prehensiveness and sufficiency. A rationale is said to be comprehensive when it

captures all the relevant information from the input text necessary for making an
accurate prediction. However, it is considered sufficient if it includes just enough
of the relevant details to still enable a correct prediction.
The comprehensiveness score evaluates how much the model’s prediction

changes when the rationale is removed from the input text; higher scores in-
dicate better comprehensiveness. Meanwhile, the sufficiency score measures how

much the model’s prediction changes when it only has access to the selected
rationale, rather than the full input; lower scores reflect better sufficiency.
5.2 Plausibility
The plausibility of rationale is assessed by comparing the degree of overlap
of rationales generated by the model and those annotated by humans. The
intersection-over-union F1 score, the F1 score, the recall, and the precision are
utilized to assess the plausibility. The setup proposed in Paranjape et al. [12]
and DeYoung et al. [11] makes use of a partial match threshold of 0.1 to account
for near matches between model-generated and human-annotated rationales.
6 Datasets

Previously, research on unsupervised selective rationalization was conducted us-
ing a de-correlated subset of the BeerAdvocate review data set McAuley et al.

[13], which had been preprocessed by Lei et al. [1]. However, at the request of
BeerAdvocate, the data set was recently removed, making it inaccessible to the

research community. The BeerAdvocate data set consisted of 80,000 labeled re-
views without rationales for training and validation, along with approximately

1,000 labeled reviews containing token-level annotated rationales for testing. In
contrast, other available data sets provide rational labels for all samples DeYoung
et al. [11] or do not offer rational labels at all Maas et al. [14]. Moreover, large
datasets such as MultiRC or FEVER typically offer sentence-level rationales,
whereas BeerAdvocate had provided token-level annotations.
To bridge this gap, Storek et al. [2] repurposed existing movie review datasets

to create a task comparable to beer review tasks, allowing new research on un-
supervised selective rationalization to be benchmarked against models originally

developed for BeerAdvocate data. Specifically, the smaller ERASER Movie Re-
view dataset (DeYoung et al. [11], Zaidan and Eisner [15], Pang and Lee [16]),

which includes complete token-level rationale annotations, was combined with

the Large Movie Review Dataset Maas et al. [14], which lacks such annota-
tions. The ERASER data set had been compiled from the IMDb archive of the

rec.arts.movies.reviews newsgroup Pang and Lee [16], while the Large Movie
Review Data set had been scraped from the IMDb website Maas et al. [14]. No
overlaps between the two datasets were found. For training, 40,000 reviews from

the Large Movie Review Dataset were used, 10,000 reviews set aside for valida-
tion, and 2,000 annotated examples from the ERASER Movie Review dataset

used for testing.
7 Experimental Setup
All experimental procedures were performed on a cloud workstation configured
with a 4-core CPU, 30 GB of RAM, and a GPU that offers 32 GB of VRAM.
Depending on the data set, the training duration of the proposed model varied,
with USR Movie Reviews requiring more than 7 hours. A fixed value of λ = 0.1

was assigned to the A2R closeness parameter. Optimization was carried out us-
ing the Adam algorithm, Kingma and Ba [17], with the hyperparameter set to a

learning rate of 2e-5 and a batch size of 16. Dataset-specific noise levels were ap-
plied, with injection probabilities set at 0.2 for USR selected based on validation

performance during tuning. Model training was completed over a maximum of
5 epochs for the USR datasets. The checkpoint corresponding to the minimum
validation loss was preserved.

9 Conclusion
By introducing Adaptive Noise Injection, the model was discouraged from relying

on superficial correlations instead, it allowed for exploring a richer set of expla-
nations that are more causally linked to the final prediction. This significantly

improved the model’s faithfulness and plausibility.

The current noise injection strategy relies on Term Frequency - Inverse Doc-
ument Frequency to determine which words to replace. Although this helps dis-
courage the model from overfitting frequent but uninformative tokens, it can also

inadvertently mask contextually important words despite having low TF*IDF
scores. Future work could explore task-specific or context-based strategies to
determine replacement probabilities, allowing the model to preserve meaningful
information during training.
